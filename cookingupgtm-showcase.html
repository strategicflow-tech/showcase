"Wouldn't it be great if AI could help this process?"
⚠ "Wouldn't it be great if AI could help this process?" is a rhetorical question used as a hook. The reader already knows AI can help — they subscribed to a GTM AI newsletter. The question creates no tension. The consequence-first alternative: "Here's what it looks like when it does." Every word after the question is the answer the reader already assumed. Lead with the result, not the hypothesis.
That's just what we did last month with a client who had a rather complex list import process. They had unique processes for their tradeshows and a completely different process for 3rd party webinars. Each required a set of steps that took hours of manual work to prepare by a person.
Three options to automate
1) Use a middleware tool like Make.com
2) Build a Claude Code skill
3) Build a custom app.
After further consideration, we pivoted to using Claude Code to build a custom app... After demo'ing it internally, Charlie gave me feedback: "It's really great, but it's not an AI project. You used AI to create a tool, but the tool isn't using AI to solve the problem."
"Scripts can only fix what you code them to. LLMs can infer data based on context and truly give you the lift you want from AI."

Hook — consequence first The sales team is waiting. You're triple-booked. The event list just landed in your inbox and every row needs to be cleaned, normalized, enriched, and imported before anyone can work it. This is the part of GTM ops that nobody talks about — and the part that eats the rest of your afternoon. Xander Broeffle, our Head of GTM Engineering, just shipped a Claude skill that cuts this from multiple hours to 10 minutes. Here's exactly what he built and why it works.
The problem — named precisely
List imports always come in at the worst possible moment
Tradeshow lists. Third-party webinar lists. Each with different fields, different conventions, different processes.
The manual version: hours of normalizing job titles, flagging hot leads, cleaning junk rows, mapping UTM parameters, and matching campaign statuses — all while the sales team sends the first of many "when will this be ready?" messages. Xander built a skill that handles the parts AI is actually good at, and uses declarative scripts for the parts where consistency matters more than inference.
What the skill does — outcome framing
It flags hot leads your booth team missed. It removes junk. It normalizes titles using AI inference, not a fixed list.
Scripts handle naming conventions and UTMs. AI handles the unstructured data humans get wrong.
The key architectural decision: declarative scripts for deterministic rules (UTM parameters, campaign statuses, phone normalization), Claude for the judgment calls (job title levels from unstructured context, buying signal detection in Notes fields). The result is a skill that improves over time — ask it what should be updated based on the last import and it tells you.
The result — the number that should have been the subject line
5 to 10 minutes of review. Not multiple hours.
Sales gets their leads. You make it to your 3pm meeting.
The skill includes human review to maintain data integrity — but the review takes minutes, not the import itself. Over time, as the skill learns from each interaction, even that review shrinks. The ops leader moves from doing the work to monitoring the output.

Diagnostic

What changed & why

Five structural upgrades. Each one addresses a specific bug in the original.

❌ Before
A Claude Skill For Speedier and Cleaner List Imports
Announces the solution. No consequence named. "Speedier and Cleaner" are adjectives without stakes. Could be the subject of any ops tool newsletter.
✅ After
The list came in hot. Here's how to process it in 10 minutes, not 3 hours.
Names the moment the reader is living. Delivers the result immediately. The "10 minutes, not 3 hours" contrast creates the open — every ops manager calculates what that gap means for their afternoon.
1
Subject line: the result belongs here, not in the last paragraph
"5 to 10 minutes to review the data compared to multiple hours per import" is the most consequential claim in the email. It is specific, verifiable, and immediately meaningful to every ops manager who has spent an afternoon manually processing an event list. It appears in the last paragraph — after the process narrative, the pivot story, the architectural explanation, and the screenshots. Moving the number to the subject line converts it from a reward for reading to the reason to read. Every subscriber who doesn't open the email never sees the only claim that makes the tool worth adopting.
2
Preview text: completes the subject line, doesn't announce the author
"A guest post by Xander, Head of GTM Eng at CS2!" tells the reader who wrote the email. The preview text slot has one job: continue the curiosity gap opened by the subject line. "Xander built a Claude skill that cuts list import time from hours to minutes — here's exactly how" delivers the mechanism behind the subject line's promise. The reader now knows what happened (hours to minutes), who solved it (Xander, a practitioner at CS2, not a vendor), and what they get by opening (exactly how). Author attribution belongs inside the email, not in the preview slot.
3
Opening paragraph: reader's situation leads, not newsletter's production
The original opens with "This week, we have a special issue of the newsletter because the incredible Xander Broeffle... is taking over and doing a guest post!" This is internal context about how the newsletter was produced. The reader is not subscribed to learn about CS2's guest post rotation. They subscribed to solve GTM ops problems. "The sales team is waiting. You're triple-booked. The event list just landed in your inbox" names the reader's situation in the first sentence. Recognition precedes explanation — always. The guest post credit belongs after the hook, not before it.
4
Rhetorical question replaced with the answer
"Wouldn't it be great if AI could help this process?" is a hypothesis directed at a reader who is already subscribed to a GTM AI newsletter. They know AI can help. The question creates no tension and buys no time — it delays the answer the reader already assumed. The rebuild replaces the question with the consequence: "Here's what it looks like when it does." Every word after the question in the original is the answer. Lead with the answer. The hypothesis is implicit in the problem statement that precedes it.
5
Section structure: outcome framing replaces process narrative
The original structures the content as a development story: we tried middleware, we built a web app, Charlie gave feedback, we pivoted, here is what we built. This is the correct sequence for a postmortem — it is not the correct sequence for an email to a busy ops manager. The rebuild restructures around three outcomes: the problem named precisely, what the skill does (with the architectural distinction that makes it trustworthy), and the result with the number. The development story is valuable context — it belongs inside the email for readers who want it, not as the primary narrative structure. Consequence before process, always.

This is the Strategic Flow method

Every word earns its place. The best number in the email belongs in the subject line. The preview text completes the subject — it never announces the author. The opening names the reader's situation, not the newsletter's production. Rhetorical questions delay the answer the reader already assumed. Structure follows outcome, not process narrative.
