How this was read

The seven Decision Friction checks were applied to web copy instead of email. The hero headline stands in for the subject line, the hero paragraph for the lead, and the section headings for the feature to outcome check. The score is structural: the order in which information reaches the reader. It is not a design, SEO or deliverability score.

Scope and limits. Twelve mobile screenshots of the homepage and the full text of the latest post. Gaps between screenshots were not reviewed. No links were followed and no analytics were available, so nothing here is a funnel number.

Proof · Zero/Buried Social Proof · Implied Transformation

Four impact numbers. No baseline, no customer, no time window.

The block titled "See measurable impact" carries four figures and nothing measurable about them. There is no company, no team size, no period and no before value. A reader who wants to trust "87% fewer status calls" has no way to ask: 87% of what, for whom, over how long?

Homepage · impact block

"3-5 hours save per week"

"50% fewer follow-up meetings"

"40% more focus time"

"87% fewer status calls"

The savings promise also changes between pages. The homepage says 3-5 hours a week. The related reading on the blog post promises to cut 6 to 10 hours of meetings a week in one title, and to go from 8 hours to under 4 in another. The article's own example goal, 18 hours down to 12, is a sixth. A buyer comparing the pages finds three promises and no method behind any of them.

The "18hrs" stat has a second problem. The homepage presents it as time lost per team member. The article says knowledge workers at 150+ person companies spend 15 to 25 hours a week in meetings, with no source, and uses 18 to 12 as a hypothetical goal. "Spent in meetings" and "lost" are different claims, and the site treats them as one.

Homepage · crisis block

"18hrs lost per team member weekly"

Blog post · goal example

"Reduce average meeting hours per person from 18 to 12"

The article teaches the standard the homepage misses. It says "Improve meeting culture" is not a key result and asks for a baseline, a target and a date. The proof block has none of the three.

CTAs and pricing · Guest Language CTA

Three verbs, one gate: every path ends in a sales conversation.

The homepage asks for "Book demo" in the nav and again under the hero. Each of the three tiers ends in "Request pricing" with no price on it. The blog post closes its product section with "Request access to Spry" and its footer block with "Book demo". Three action names cover what is one funnel, and each one places the reader outside the action, waiting for the company to respond.

Starter, Growth, Enterprise: "Request pricing"

The nav also holds "Meeting Cost Calculator", the one asset that delivers the homepage promise ("Put a price on time") in seconds. It sits as a text link at the same weight as "Blog". Neither hero CTA points to it. A reader with thirty seconds of curiosity has one visible route: talk to sales.

The tier copy adds no way to self-select. Starter is "For small teams ready to cut wasted time." Growth is "For scaling orgs with more complexity." Enterprise is "Advanced security & integrations." No seat count, no feature difference, no starting price.

The article does one thing right here. "See your team's meeting load" is ownership language: the reader performs the action. The next words, "Request access to Spry", hand the reader back to a queue.

Demo data · Missing Visual Hierarchy

The dashboards contradict their own story.

Spry's promise is accurate time accounting. The demo screens do not pass a simple check. None of the demo data is labeled as illustrative.

  • Arrow colors invert the message. Meetings time 18% carries a green up arrow (+12%). Transition time 33% and Buffers 43% carry red down arrows. The product's claim is less wasted time, and the chart colors the reductions as bad news.
  • The donut does not match its legend. The ring has four colors and the legend names three. The headline number, Deep work 15%, has no legend entry. The three labeled shares add to 94%. Adding the 15% headline gives 109%.
  • Durations disagree with clock times. "Reserve slot 8:30-9:30am" carries a 1:30 badge, which is 60 minutes. "Monday, sep 19, 7-8:30am" carries 0:30, which is 90 minutes.
  • Dates do not fit one calendar. "Monday, sep 12" fits 2022. "Wed, May 12" fits 2021 and 2027. Neither matches this year, where 12 September is a Saturday.
  • The time axis skips and truncates. Weekly buckets read Jun 17-23, Jun 24-30, Jul 8-14, then a two-day bucket, Jul 15-16. The week of Jul 1-7 is missing.
  • Cards do not connect. The Dev team card shows "$ 1,542" cost per period. The insights card shows Total time "4,400h". If both cover the same period, that prices an hour at about 35 cents.

Blog post · Ways of Working Template, 10 Jun 2026

The article serves the reader, then loses them at the handoff.

The opening is reader-first and specific: a new hire arrives with old habits and books a 30-minute sync for a Slack message. The six norms are concrete. The post earns attention. The structure then works against the product.

The template position contradicts itself

The intro says the free template is at the bottom. The next section says here it is, and later the text calls it the template at the top of the page. A reader who scans for the download and finds it early has no reason to reach the product section.

Blog post · intro vs first section

"The free template is at the bottom."

"If you're here for the template, here it is."

The product bridge arrives last

"How Spry Helps You See If It's Working" comes after the template, six norms, goals, three document types, the personal version and before the FAQ. The reader who came for the template has already left. The bridge also leads with mechanics: calendar analytics, hours in meetings, recurring meeting cost.

The second template has no download

The "How to Work With Me" section lists six items and offers no file, link or CTA. The only download in the text is the company guide.

The buyer changes mid-article

The FAQ says the guide belongs to the COO, Head of People or a founder. The product section says "Spry gives managers the data". The stat is about companies of 150+ people, while the homepage opens with a Dev team and a Starter tier for small teams. Three audiences appear in one page.

The article never points to the calculator

A post about meeting overload, on a site with a free Meeting Cost Calculator in the nav, does not mention the calculator anywhere in the body text reviewed.

Title is a filing label

"Ways of Working Template (Free, 2026)" names the topic and the year. It works as a search title. It gives the reader no consequence for the meeting problem the article itself describes so well.

Email-to-Product Handoff · UX-level read

The promise is "see your team's meeting load". The visible route is a sales call.

Read type: UX-level, from screenshots and article text. Not instrumented. No analytics access was available, links were not followed, and no funnel numbers are stated.

  • Does the destination match the promise? Not verifiable. The article promises "See your team's meeting load". The CTA that follows says "Request access to Spry". The destination behind it was not visible: a demo form, a waitlist and a signup would each change the verdict.
  • Is the action name consistent? No. The same funnel uses "Book demo", "Request pricing" and "Request access to Spry". A reader who clicked one and saw the other would re-orient.
  • Is the fastest route to value linked? The Meeting Cost Calculator exists in the nav. The article body does not link to it, and the hero does not lead to it.
  • Deep link behavior: not checkable without following the links. Left open.

What already works

The hero, the rule and the goal format are strong. Keep them.

  • "Every hour has a cost." Consequence before product, in five words. It is the strongest line on the site.
  • "Calendars are full, yet results don't always grow." The reader's situation arrives before the company's news.
  • "No agenda? No meeting." A rule stated in four words, and the article backs it with a full section on when to meet.
  • The goal format in the article. "Reduce average meeting hours per person from 18 to 12 per week by end of quarter" has a baseline, a target and a deadline. It is the standard the site's own proof block needs to meet.
  • "Put a price on time." An outcome-shaped step name, and the calculator behind it is a real free asset.
  • The article opening. A recognizable scene, named cost, no jargon.
Cross-section synthesis

The Actual Gap: the site sells measured time and shows unmeasured claims.

Every finding traces to one root cause. The product's promise is measurement, and the pages do not demonstrate measurement anywhere the reader looks.

ProofNumbers arrive without a baseline, customer or period, and the savings figure changes from page to page.
Demo dataThe dashboards that should show accuracy fail arithmetic, color logic and date checks.
CTAsThe one asset that lets a reader test the promise, the calculator, is a nav link. Every button leads to a call.
Blog postThe article teaches baselines and targets, then hands the reader to a product section that has neither.
HandoffThree action names, an unverified destination, and no path from the article to the calculator.

A reader reaching "See measurable impact" has one question: measured where, on whose team, over what period? The page does not answer it, and the next click is a sales conversation. That is where the structural score loses its points.
